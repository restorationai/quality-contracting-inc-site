Flood damage behaves differently than a single burst pipe: the water usually enters from outside, through a foundation crack, a failed sump pump, or storm runoff that overwhelms the grading around your home, and it often carries silt, lawn chemicals, or sewage backflow with it. That changes how the materials are treated and what has to come out of the structure versus what can be dried in place.

## What flood damage restoration actually involves

Flood damage restoration covers the extraction, drying, and sanitizing of a structure after water enters from an external source rather than a plumbing failure. Because floodwater is classified as contaminated (Category 3) in almost every case, the work isn't just pulling water out with a pump. Porous materials that absorbed that water, carpet, pad, drywall up to the waterline, batt insulation, often can't be saved and have to be removed rather than dried. Crews use truck-mounted or portable extraction units first, then set up axial fans and low-grain-refrigerant dehumidifiers sized to the cubic footage of the affected space, not just the square footage. Monitoring continues over several days with moisture meters and thermal imaging to confirm that wall cavities, subfloor, and framing have actually released their moisture, not just the surface.

## Our process

1. **Safety and contamination assessment.** Before anything else, the crew checks for electrical hazards, structural compromise, and confirms the water category so the right PPE and containment protocols are used.
2. **Bulk water extraction.** Standing water is pumped and vacuumed out, prioritizing rooms with the deepest water and any areas where electrical systems are at risk.
3. **Removal of unsalvageable materials.** Saturated drywall, baseboards, carpet, pad, and insulation that contacted contaminated water are cut out and bagged for disposal, typically to a consistent height above the visible waterline.
4. **Structural drying with daily monitoring.** Dehumidifiers and air movers run until moisture readings in framing and subfloor return to a dry standard, with readings logged to document progress for the insurance file.
5. **Antimicrobial application and pre-reconstruction verification.** Affected framing and remaining materials are treated, and the space is checked before any drywall, flooring, or trim goes back in.

## What separates a good flood response from a bad one

The most common mistake is drying a flooded space without removing the materials that should have come out. Carpet pad, for example, almost never survives contact with floodwater, and drying it in place just traps contamination between the subfloor and the carpet. Insulation behaves the same way: once wet, fiberglass batt loses its R-value and holds moisture against the framing long after the surface looks dry.

Adjusters reviewing a flood claim look for moisture logs showing day-by-day readings, photos of material removal before drying began, and documentation of the water source and category. A rushed job that skips this paperwork, or dries over contaminated material to save time, tends to resurface as a mold claim within a few weeks. Hidden moisture in sill plates and joist bays is the other place corners get cut; it takes longer to dry and is easy to miss without thermal imaging.

## What does flood damage restoration cost?

Flood restoration costs scale with how much water entered, how long it sat, and how much material has to be removed rather than just dried. These are typical industry ranges, not a quote: every loss is different, and Quality Contracting, Inc. provides a written scope of work before any work begins. Homeowners insurance typically does not cover flood damage from external water sources unless you carry a separate flood policy (often through NFIP), while damage from an internal source like a failed sump pump may fall under your standard policy, so it's worth confirming coverage type before assuming what's paid.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Auburn and the surrounding Worcester County towns see two distinct flood risk windows: spring snowmelt combined with rain-on-frozen-ground runoff, and late-summer storm systems that can drop a lot of rain fast on saturated soil. Many homes in this area have older fieldstone or block foundations that were never fully waterproofed, which means seepage during heavy rain is common even without a dramatic storm event. Freeze-thaw cycles through a New England winter can also open new cracks in foundations that were fine the year before, so a basement that's never flooded can start taking on water after a hard winter.
