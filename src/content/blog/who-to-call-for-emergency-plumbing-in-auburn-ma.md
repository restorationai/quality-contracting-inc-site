## When is it a plumber versus a restoration company?

If water is actively flowing and damage is already spreading, call a restoration-capable emergency plumber, not a plumber-only shop, so stopping the leak and starting mitigation happen in the same call. A plumber-only outfit fixes the pipe and leaves; it won't extract the standing water, pull wet baseboards, or document the loss for your insurer.

Use this rough breakdown:

- **Water still running, flooring or walls affected:** call an emergency plumbing and restoration team so the leak gets stopped and the drying starts the same visit.
- **Gas smell, sparking outlets, or water near an electrical panel:** call 911 or the fire department first, then National Grid or your utility for a gas or electrical shutoff, before anyone touches the water.
- **Slow drip with no active flooding:** a standard plumber during business hours may be all you need, though it's worth having it checked before it becomes an overnight failure.
- **Water has already dried but you're dealing with the insurance side:** that conversation typically starts after a mitigation company has documented the loss, not before.

Most homeowners insurance policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a supply line that bursts without warning, but they draw a hard line at gradual leaks or deferred maintenance, so getting someone on-site fast to stop and document the loss protects the claim (see the [Insurance Information Institute's overview of what a standard policy covers](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance)). ## What happens when you call?

The dispatcher asks a short set of questions: where the water's coming from, whether you've shut off the source, and whether it's affecting flooring, drywall, or anything electrical. Have your address, the approximate start time of the leak, and your insurance carrier's name ready if you have it.

A technician arrives with a moisture meter, shop vacuums or truck-mounted extraction equipment, and the parts or tools needed to address common emergency plumbing failures on the spot. They'll shut off the source if it isn't already off, assess what's wet, and start extraction before moving to drying equipment. If the water category requires it (a sewage backup, for example, is treated differently than a clean supply-line break), they'll flag that immediately since it changes how the cleanup proceeds.

If the job involves rebuilding anything that got cut out, drywall, flooring, cabinetry, that's where the "One Call Does It All" part matters: the same team that stopped the leak can also handle the reconstruction, so you're not lining up a separate contractor three weeks later.

## What should I do before help arrives?

Shut off the water at the fixture or the main shutoff valve, then move anything you can off the wet floor. If it's safe to do so, open a window or run a box fan to start air movement, and take photos of the water and any damaged items before you start cleaning anything up, since those photos matter for your claim.

Don't run a wet/dry vac near standing water if there's any chance it's near an outlet or panel. Don't wait until morning hoping it'll dry on its own; mold can begin growing on wet materials within 24 to 48 hours according to the [EPA's guidance on mold and moisture](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home), so the clock matters more than it feels like it does at midnight.
