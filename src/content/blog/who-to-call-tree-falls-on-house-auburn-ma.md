A downed oak or white pine across a roofline after a nor'easter or a heavy wet snow is one of the most common storm calls in Central Massachusetts. The confusion is usually about order: do you call a tree company first, or a restoration contractor, or your insurance agent? The honest answer is it depends on what's actually happened to the house, which is why the next few minutes matter more than the next few hours.

## Is It Safe to Stay in the House Right Now?

Leave the house immediately if you smell gas, see sparking wires, hear cracking from the attic, or the tree has punched through to a room below. Those are signs of a compromised structure or a live electrical hazard, and neither waits for a contractor.

If anyone is injured, or a power line is down anywhere near the tree, call 911 first. Downed lines can be live even if they look dead, and a partially-collapsed roof can continue to settle for hours after the initial strike.

If the tree only clipped the roof edge, took out a gutter, or is resting on the structure without visible interior damage, it is usually safe to stay, but keep everyone out of the room directly under the impact point until someone has looked at the framing. Shut off power to that part of the house at the breaker if water is also coming in, since wet drywall and live wiring do not mix.

## Who Do You Call First, a Tree Service or a Restoration Contractor?

Call a tree removal company to get the tree itself off the structure, and call a restoration contractor to protect and repair the house. These are two different trades doing two different jobs, and most homeowners only need to make one of those calls personally.

A tree service brings the equipment to cut and lift a tree safely, especially when it is still under tension against a roofline. A restoration contractor like QCI Restoration handles everything that happens to the house once the tree is off: emergency tarping, drying out any water intrusion, structural assessment, and the full repair. Many restoration companies either keep a tree-removal crew on call or coordinate directly with one, which means one phone call can set both trucks in motion instead of you tracking down two separate vendors while it's raining into your living room.

If utility lines are tangled in the branches, the utility company has to clear them before anyone touches the tree. Do not attempt to cut branches near a line yourself, and do not let a tree crew work near one until the utility has confirmed it's de-energized.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover a Tree Falling on Your House?

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden damage from a falling tree, including the cost to remove the part of the tree that's actually on the structure, and the repair work that follows. [Homeowners insurance generally covers wind and falling-object damage](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance) as a named peril, which a tree strike during a storm typically qualifies as.

Whose tree it was usually matters less than most people think. In Massachusetts, if a healthy tree falls during a storm, your own policy typically pays for your damage regardless of whose yard the tree grew in, since the cause was a storm event rather than negligence. If you believe the tree was already dead, diseased, or previously flagged as a hazard, that can shift liability toward the tree's owner, but that is a legal and insurance determination, not something a contractor can tell you on-site. Loop your adjuster in early and let your insurer and, if needed, an attorney sort out liability while repairs move forward.

What protects the claim either way is documentation: photos of the tree, the point of impact, and any interior water intrusion before anything gets moved or cleaned up. A written scope of work from the contractor doing the tarping and repair also gives the adjuster something concrete to work from.

## What Happens During Emergency Tarping and the Roof Repair?

Emergency tarping happens first, usually within hours of the call, to stop active water intrusion while the tree is still being dealt with. A crew measures the opening, cuts and secures heavy-gauge poly or canvas tarping anchored into the roof deck, and checks the attic and interior for water that's already found its way in.

Once the tree is removed and the structure is stable, the real assessment starts: how much of the roof deck, rafters, or trusses were damaged, whether the damage is contained to the roof or extends into the ceiling and walls below, and what the rebuild actually requires. Smaller strikes might mean a section of shingles, sheathing, and a patched ceiling. A direct hit through the roof deck can mean replacing rafters, running new roofing, and rebuilding drywall and insulation underneath, drying everything to the IICRC S500 standard first if water got into the framing.

This is also where the "one call does it all" part matters most for homeowners. Instead of coordinating a roofer, a drywall contractor, a painter, and a general contractor separately, a single restoration company can carry the job from the tarp to the finished rebuild under one scope and one point of contact. That's the whole idea behind QCI Restoration's approach: one call covers the tarp, the drying, the roofing, and the reconstruction, so you're not managing four different vendors and four different schedules while your roof has a hole in it.

Before hiring anyone for permanent repairs, confirm they're a registered Massachusetts home improvement contractor. The state keeps a [Home Improvement Contractor Program](https://www.mass.gov/home-improvement-contractor-program) specifically so homeowners can verify this before signing anything.
