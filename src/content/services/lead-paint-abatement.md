If your home was built before 1978, the paint on your trim, windows, and walls likely contains lead, and that becomes a real problem the moment it starts to chip, chalk, or get disturbed by a renovation. A window that sticks and grinds paint dust into the sash channel every time it's opened, a porch railing shedding flakes into the dirt where kids play, a sanding project that turns old paint into airborne dust: these are the scenarios that turn a dormant hazard into an active one. Quality Contracting, Inc. handles the testing, containment, and removal work that gets a property back to safe before anyone starts breathing or tracking lead dust through the house.

## What Lead Paint Abatement actually involves

Lead paint abatement is the controlled removal or permanent enclosure of lead-based paint and the dust it generates, not just a fresh coat of paint over the problem. Depending on the substrate, that means wet-scraping and hand-sanding with HEPA-attached tools, chemical stripping, full component removal (an entire window unit or door, rather than disturbing it in place), or encapsulation with a lead-rated coating where removal isn't practical. Work areas get sealed in plastic sheeting with negative air where the scope calls for it, and every surface gets cleaned with HEPA vacuums and lead-specific cleaning agents before the containment comes down. For a typical single-room job this runs a few days; whole-house projects involving multiple rooms, exterior siding, or trim can stretch to a couple of weeks depending on how much surface area is affected and how it's layered with other coatings.

## Our process

1. **Lead paint testing.** Paint chip samples or XRF testing identify which components and rooms actually contain lead, so work isn't done (or skipped) on guesswork.
2. **Containment setup.** Plastic sheeting, floor protection, and negative air equipment isolate the work area from the rest of the living space before any paint is disturbed.
3. **Removal or encapsulation.** Depending on the substrate and condition, crews use wet methods, HEPA-filtered sanding, component removal, or encapsulating coatings, always working wet or HEPA-filtered to minimize airborne dust.
4. **HEPA cleanup and clearance.** Surfaces, floors, and containment areas are HEPA-vacuumed and wet-wiped in sequence, following lead-safe renovation cleaning protocols, before containment is removed.
5. **Dust wipe testing.** A final dust sample or visual clearance check confirms the space meets the standard for re-occupancy before furniture and residents move back in.

## What separates a good lead paint abatement response from a bad one

The most common failure in lead work isn't the removal itself, it's containment that leaks. Dry-sanding without HEPA attachment, propping open containment doors for convenience, or skipping floor protection in adjacent rooms are the shortcuts that turn a contained job into a dust event that spreads lead into carpets, HVAC returns, and places it's much harder to clean out of. A good crew treats containment as non-negotiable, uses wet methods or HEPA-shrouded tools on every cut and scrape, and documents the work with photos and dust wipe results rather than just saying it's done. Work on pre-1978 housing also falls under the EPA RRP (Renovation, Repair, and Painting) rule, and following that rule's work practice standards, like wet sanding and the posted warning signage, isn't optional on applicable projects. Insurance adjusters and property managers reviewing a claim or a tenant complaint will usually ask for the testing results, the containment photos, and the clearance documentation, not just a verbal assurance the job is finished.

## What does Lead Paint Abatement cost?

Costs vary widely based on how many components are affected, whether removal or encapsulation is appropriate, and whether the project is a single window or a full exterior repaint. Every project is different, which is why Quality Contracting, Inc. provides a written scope of work, based on testing results, before any abatement work begins. Homeowners insurance typically does not cover routine lead paint abatement unless the hazard is tied to a covered loss, such as fire or water damage that exposed lead-painted substrates, so this is often an out-of-pocket or grant-funded project rather than a claims-based one.
