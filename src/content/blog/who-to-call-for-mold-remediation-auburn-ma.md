## Do You Need a Mold Inspector or a Remediation Company First?

It depends on the size of the problem and whether you already know the cause. The [EPA's mold guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) notes that patches smaller than about 10 square feet, such as mold on a bathroom tile grout line, can often be cleaned by a homeowner with detergent and water and proper ventilation. Anything larger, anything hidden inside a wall cavity, or anything tied to a basement leak calls for a professional.

A mold inspector and a mold remediation company are not the same service. An inspector runs air sampling or surface swabs and writes a report, without doing any cleanup, which avoids a conflict of interest if you need neutral documentation for an insurance claim or a home sale. A remediation company sets up containment, removes affected material, and runs the drying equipment. If the mold is visible and you already know it came from a specific leak, you usually don't need to pay for testing first; the EPA's position is that visible mold calls for cleanup regardless of what species it is. Our guide on [how to test for mold](/blog/how-to-test-for-mold/) walks through when a standalone test is worth the cost.

## What If the Mold Started After a Basement Leak?

Fix the water source before the mold gets treated, every time. The S520 standard for mold remediation is clear that treating growth without correcting the ongoing moisture just means it comes back within weeks. A lot of Auburn's older capes and colonials sit on fieldstone or block basement foundations that were never built to modern waterproofing standards, and groundwater seepage after a heavy rain is one of the most common causes of basement mold in this area.

That's why a mold job and a leak-detection job usually need to happen together. A crew that only treats the surface mold without tracking down where the water is entering is setting you up for a repeat call in a year. Our [signs of hidden mold](/blog/signs-of-hidden-mold/) post covers the warning signs, like a musty smell with no visible source, that mean there's moisture intrusion somewhere you haven't found yet.

## What Happens When You Call for Mold Remediation?

The first call is mostly information-gathering: how big the affected area looks, where it is in the house, how long the smell or staining has been there, and whether there's a known leak or past flooding event. Having a few photos ready on your phone speeds this up.

On arrival, a tech reads moisture levels with a meter against the drywall or foundation wall and checks relative humidity in the room before touching anything. If the scope matches what was discussed on the phone, the next steps generally run in this order:

1. **Containment** - poly sheeting seals off the work area so spores don't spread to the rest of the house during removal.
2. **Removal** - porous material that's actively growing mold (drywall, insulation, some carpet) gets cut out and bagged; non-porous surfaces get HEPA-vacuumed and treated.
3. **Antimicrobial treatment** - remaining surfaces are treated to address spores the removal step didn't reach.
4. **Drying** - air movers and a dehumidifier run until the area reads back to a normal moisture range, because a damp wall cavity is how the problem restarts.
5. **Clearance check** - a final moisture reading and visual check before containment comes down.

On the insurance side, Massachusetts homeowners policies generally follow the same pattern most states use: mold tied to a sudden, covered event, like a burst supply line, is usually covered as part of that claim, while mold from a long-standing leak or deferred maintenance typically is not. The [Massachusetts mold page](https://www.mass.gov/doc/mold-cleanup-repairs-and-excessive-moisture-control-a-step-by-step-guide-for-homeowners/download) has the state's consumer guidance if you want the plain-language version before you call your carrier.

If the job involves more than the mold itself, say the leak also damaged flooring or drywall that needs rebuilding, that's the advantage of making one call instead of three. One Call Does It All means the same crew that finds and fixes the moisture source can also handle the remediation and the rebuild, so you're not coordinating a plumber, a mold company, and a general contractor separately.
